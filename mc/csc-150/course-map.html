Course companion

The Learning Hub

One page per course, open all term, never graded and never submitted. It is where the parts of the design that span lessons actually live.

Vocabulary - 56 terms

Every term carries a plain-language definition, a one-line why it matters, and the lesson it arrives in. Terms from lessons the learner has marked done are highlighted; nothing is ever hidden, because a glossary you cannot look ahead in is not a glossary. Practice runs through the same flashcard deck as the lesson warm-ups - definition first, four term options, drawn only from covered terms. Vocabulary is part of the spacing system rather than a sidebar to it.

Reflection

Twelve rotating weekly prompts plus five module checkpoints - guided, because blank boxes get blank answers. Entries are dated and kept, and the whole journal exports as plain text. The prompts ask about specific moments (“where did you catch yourself trusting an output you shouldn’t have?”) rather than for general impressions.

Confidence over time

The four sliders from the Week 1 inventory, re-takeable whenever. Two or more checkpoints render a first–to–latest readout per outcome with the delta. This is the longitudinal half of the pre/post pair, and the single most interesting number the course produces.

Progress

Nineteen tiles, learner-marked. Doubles as the gate for which vocabulary counts as covered. In the LMS this would read real completion - but deciding you are done with something is its own small act of reflection, so the manual version is worth keeping either way.

Course tool

Tokenizer

The second standing page. Introduced in Lesson 2 and returned to whenever the cost of text matters.

What it does

Paste anything; see it split into real cl100k_base tokens - the actual OpenAI vocabulary bundled into the page, running locally, with nothing sent anywhere. Chips alternate tint so boundaries read, spaces are marked so learners can see that the space belongs to the token after it, and multi-token characters carry a badge showing what they really cost. A live read-out interprets the specific text in front of them rather than reciting general facts about tokens.

On honesty: the page states plainly that this is one vendor’s tokenizer and that Claude, Gemini and Llama will give different counts - the patterns hold everywhere, the numbers do not. Shipping a plausible-looking approximation in a course about not trusting plausible-looking output would have undercut the whole syllabus, which is why the real vocabulary is bundled instead.

01 What This Even Is The Nesting Dolls CLO 2CLO 3
Summary

Almost everyone arrives at this course thinking AI means the chatbot. That is a reasonable thing to think and it will get in your way, because the tools you spend the next fifteen weeks evaluating are one narrow branch of something much older and much wider. This lesson sorts the vocabulary out - what counts as AI, what machine learning has to do with it, where the chatbot actually sits inside all of it - and answers the question everybody has and nobody asks: if this field is seventy years old, why did it only start mattering recently? You are not expected to know any of this yet. That is the point of putting it first.

02 The Next Word Machine Token Roulette CLO 2CLO 3
Summary

Everything else in this course rests on one idea: a language model produces text one piece at a time, choosing whatever is most likely to come next. It is not looking anything up. Here you watch that process happen - building a sentence yourself from the model’s own ranked options, then turning a dial that decides how adventurous those choices get. By the end you will be able to say what the machine is doing without using the word think.

Token Roulette

The learner builds a sentence by choosing from probability-ranked continuations, watching the bars reshape at each step. A temperature slider flattens or sharpens the distribution, and running the same stem three times at high temperature produces three different sentences - which answers the prediction question with the learner’s own hands rather than a paragraph of explanation.

Course toolStanding, not lesson-bound

Tokenizer

Embedded in this lesson’s first content segment, and standing on its own as a course tool for Lessons 5, 14 and 15. A live read-out names what makes that particular text cheap or expensive: characters per token, tokens per word, the priciest single word, how much the emoji cost, and how many tokens begin with a space. Students type their own name, then a classmate’s; paste an English paragraph, then its Spanish translation. The fairness point lands harder as arithmetic than as assertion.

03 Why It Wanders Attention Spotlight CLO 2CLO 3
Summary

Last lesson your token machine produced a sentence that started confidently and drifted into nonsense by the end of the line. That was not a flaw in the demonstration. It is the honest behavior of something with a two-word memory, and repairing it is the single change that separates the models you use now from everything that came before them. This lesson is about attention - letting the machine look at every word at once and work out for itself which ones matter. It is the one piece of architecture worth knowing by name, and you already have the question it answers.

Lesson outcomes

Attention Spotlight

The trophy-and-suitcase sentence with one word switchable between big and small. The learner clicks the pronoun and the sentence lights up to show which words carry weight in resolving it; flipping the adjective moves the spotlight to the other noun with nothing else in the sentence touched. Prompt Surgery

Index D

Vocabulary, by lesson

All 70 terms in CSC 150, in the order a learner meets them. Every lesson introduces at least one. Each term carries a plain-language definition and a one-line why it matters - the second line is the one that does the work, because a definition nobody can use is just a word with a longer word after it.

70 terms
Lesson 01 · What This Even Is

9 terms

Artificial intelligence (AI)L01 · M1

The whole field: any system built to do something that would need intelligence if a person did it. Seventy years old, and most of it is not what you think.

The broadest word on this list and the least useful on its own - always ask which kind.

Machine learningL01 · M1

Systems that derive their behavior from data rather than from rules a person wrote out.

The shift that made most modern AI possible: nobody hand-wrote the rules for spotting spam.

Deep learningL01 · M1

Machine learning built on neural networks with many layers. The inner ring where language models live.

Explains why these systems are hard to inspect - nobody wrote the intermediate steps.

Neural networkL01 · M1

A stack of numerical layers loosely inspired by neurons, adjusted during training until its outputs match the data.

The mechanism underneath every tool in this course, and older than most people assume. For more information, watch Terminator 2.

Narrow AIL01 · M1

A system good at one bounded task and useless outside it. Every tool in this course.

Cuts through most of the hype in one move.

General AI (AGI)L01 · M1

A hypothetical system matching human flexibility across arbitrary tasks. Does not exist.

Worth naming precisely so you can tell when a headline is describing something else.

Generative AIL01 · M1

The branch that produces new text, images or audio rather than sorting or scoring existing things.

Distinguishes the chatbot from the spam filter, which is the distinction most coverage skips.

AI winterL01 · M1

One of two stretches - roughly 1974 to 1980, and 1987 to 1993 - when research funding and public interest in AI collapsed after the field promised far more than it delivered.

The reason to be careful with any claim about what is about to happen next. Confident, specific predictions about AI have been made before, and twice the field had to spend a decade recovering from them.

Large language model (LLM)L01 · M1

A system trained on an enormous amount of text to predict what comes next. Everything else it appears to do follows from that one job.

If you keep one definition from this course, keep this one - most of Module 3 is a consequence of it.

Lesson 02 · The Next Word Machine

5 terms

TokenL02 · M1

The chunk of text a model actually works with - usually a word, a piece of a word, or a punctuation mark. “Unbelievable” might be three tokens.

Cost, speed and context limits are all measured in tokens, never in words.

TrainingL02 · M1

Building the model, in stages, before anyone could use it - first predicting text across a huge corpus, then being tuned by people toward answering usefully.

All of it finished before you ever typed anything. The model is not learning from your conversation, whatever it sounds like.

InferenceL02 · M1

What happens when you press enter: the finished model generating one token at a time.

Explains why the identical prompt can come back different twice in a row.

TemperatureL02 · M1

A setting controlling how strongly the model favors the single most likely next token over a less likely one.

Low is repetitive and safe, high is inventive and unreliable. Most chat tools hide this.

ParametersL02 · M1

The numbers inside the model that training adjusted. Sizes are quoted in billions of them.

The number marketing leans on hardest, and a poor predictor of whether a model suits your task.

Lesson 03 · Why It Wanders

3 terms

AttentionL03 · M1

The mechanism that lets a model weigh every word in the input at once and decide which ones bear on the word it is producing.

The answer to why the token machine wandered and current models do not.

TransformerL03 · M1

The architecture built on attention, introduced in 2017. Effectively every model in this course is one.

The single piece of architecture worth knowing by name - the T in GPT.

Context (long-range)L03 · M1

Material far from the current word that still determines its meaning.

What a two-word memory cannot hold and attention can - and why paragraphs hold together now.

Lesson 04 · Anatomy of a Prompt

5 terms

PromptL04 · M1

Everything you send in one turn - the question plus whatever context, examples and instructions come with it.

Most of what gets blamed on “bad AI” is a prompt problem.

ContextL04 · M1

The material you hand the model to work from: pasted text, uploaded files, earlier turns.

Grounding a request in real context is the single largest quality lever you control.

Few-shot (worked example)L04 · M1

Showing the model one or more examples of the output you want rather than describing it.

Far more reliable than adjectives for pinning down a format.

System promptL04 · M1

Standing instructions sitting behind a conversation that shape every reply in it.

How custom assistants get their rules - and the foundation of CSC 240.

ConstraintL04 · M1

A hard limit written into the prompt: word count, reading level, what to leave out, what to never say.

Constraints do real work. “Be good” does none.

Lesson 05 · The Landscape

7 terms

Context windowL05 · M1

The maximum amount of text a model can hold in view at once, your prompt and its reply together.

Go past it and the earliest material silently drops out of view - no warning.

Knowledge cutoffL05 · M1

The date after which a model’s training data stops. It knows nothing later unless a tool fetches it.

The single most common cause of a confidently wrong answer about anything current.

MultimodalL05 · M1

A model that handles more than text - images, audio, sometimes video, as input or output.

Decides whether you can paste a screenshot at all.

Chat modelL05 · M1

The default kind: it answers in one pass, immediately, and is the right tool for most things.

The one you are already using. It is the baseline the other two are being compared against, which is why it needs a name.

Reasoning modelL05 · M1

A model that spends extra computation working through a problem in steps before answering.

Slower and costlier. Worth it for multi-step problems, wasted on a rewrite.

Foundation modelL05 · M1

A large general-purpose model that other products are built on top of.

Several tools you use may be the same model wearing different clothes.

Rate limitL05 · M1

A cap on how much you can use a tool in a given window, usually much tighter on free tiers.

Find yours before an assignment is due, not during.

Lesson 06 · Choosing a Model, Blind

4 terms

BenchmarkL06 · M2

A standard test used to compare models on one specific skill.

Models can be tuned to score well on benchmarks without being better at your actual work.

LeaderboardL06 · M2

A ranked table of models by benchmark score or human preference.

Genuinely useful and routinely over-read.

Blind comparisonL06 · M2

Rating two outputs without knowing which model produced either.

The only way to measure the answer instead of the brand.

Elo ratingL06 · M2

A ranking score borrowed from chess, updated every time one option beats another head to head.

How LM Arena turns a pile of votes into an ordered list.

Lesson 07 · Iteration and Repair

5 terms

ThreadL07 · M2

One continuous conversation. Everything in it stays in context until the window fills up.

A long thread can quietly poison a new question asked inside it.

Context accumulationL07 · M2

The build-up of earlier turns, which increasingly shapes every later answer.

The reason a fresh chat sometimes beats one more follow-up.

DriftL07 · M2

A thread gradually moving away from what you originally asked, as each turn is written against everything before it.

The symptom you notice; context accumulation is the cause underneath it. Naming it is what stops you arguing with a thread you should be restarting.

RegenerationL07 · M2

Asking for another attempt at the same prompt instead of editing the prompt.

Cheap to try, and it tells you whether the problem is your prompt or the luck of the draw.

AI slopL07 · M2

Fluent, generic AI output that reads as finished work and carries almost nothing. Usually not wrong - just empty, and produced faster than anybody can read it.

The failure mode this module is for. A wrong answer gets caught eventually because it collides with something real; slop passes review because there is nothing in it to object to.

Lesson 08 · Why AI Makes Things Up

3 terms

HallucinationL08 · M2

A fluent, confident, well-formatted output that is simply untrue. “Fabrication” is the plainer word for it.

Not a malfunction. The same machinery that produces correct answers produces these.

ConfabulationL08 · M2

Filling a gap with plausible invention instead of signaling uncertainty.

Borrowed from neurology, and a more honest description than “hallucination”.

PlausibilityL08 · M2

How right something sounds. Exactly what the model optimises for, and therefore useless as a truth test.

The trap inside every fabricated citation you will ever see.

Lesson 09 · Models That Can Actually Look Things Up

3 terms

GroundingL09 · M3

Tying an answer to specific retrieved source material rather than to training alone.

The main structural defense against fabrication.

RetrievalL09 · M3

Fetching documents or search results and putting them into the model’s context before it answers.

What lets a tool discuss last week at all.

CitationL09 · M3

A reference to a source. Check two things: that it exists, and that it says what was claimed.

The second check is the one almost everybody skips.

Lesson 10 · Where Bias Comes From

3 terms

BiasL10 · M3

Systematic skew in output tracing back to training data, human feedback, or the framing of the prompt itself.

A different problem from being wrong, and it needs a different fix.

RLHFL10 · M3

Reinforcement learning from human feedback - people rate outputs, and the model is tuned toward what they preferred.

The second place bias enters, after the training data.

ProbeL10 · M3

A deliberately controlled prompt pair that changes exactly one variable to surface a difference.

Turns “it feels biased” into something you can show someone.

Lesson 11 · The Verification Habit

3 terms

VerificationL11 · M3

Independently confirming a claim before you rely on it.

The habit this entire course exists to install.

ProvenanceL11 · M3

Where a piece of content came from and what happened to it along the way.

Increasingly the thing you will be asked to document.

StakesL11 · M3

What happens if this is wrong, and how hard it would be to check.

Together these decide how much verification a task actually deserves.

Lesson 12 · Making Images

3 terms

Diffusion modelL12 · M4

The kind of model behind most image generators. It starts from noise and repeatedly refines toward your prompt.

Explains why counting, hands and text-in-images are so hard for it.

Negative promptL12 · M4

A separate field listing what you do not want. Not the same as writing “no X” in the prompt itself.

“No people” in the main prompt reliably produces people.

SeedL12 · M4

The starting random number. The same seed with the same prompt reproduces the same image.

How you change one thing without losing the whole picture.

Lesson 13 · Voice and Audio

4 terms

Speech-to-text (ASR)L13 · M4

Automatic transcription of recorded speech into written text.

Error rates are not evenly distributed across accents, names, or speakers.

Text-to-speechL13 · M4

Generating spoken audio from written text.

Good enough now that consent is a real question rather than a hypothetical.

Voice cloningL13 · M4

Producing synthetic speech in a specific person’s voice from a sample of it.

Legally and ethically the sharpest edge anywhere in this course.

Word error rate (WER)L13 · M4

The standard measure of transcription accuracy: errors divided by words spoken.

Lets you compare two tools instead of arguing about which felt better.

Lesson 14 · Working Across Modalities

3 terms

Vision inputL14 · M4

Giving a model an image - screenshot, photo, scan - as part of the prompt.

Degrades badly with skew, handwriting and dense tables, and does so silently.

OCRL14 · M4

Optical character recognition: pulling text out of an image of text.

Frequently the better tool than a vision model when the scan is clean.

ModalityL14 · M4

A kind of input or output: text, image, audio, video.

Choosing the right one is usually a bigger win than improving the prompt.

Lesson 15 · Your Data, Their Servers

5 terms

Personally identifiable information (PII)L15 · M5

Anything that identifies a specific person, on its own or combined with other data.

The category that decides what you may and may not paste.

Data retentionL15 · M5

How long a vendor keeps what you send, and what they are permitted to do with it.

Varies by tier, and lives in the settings rather than the marketing.

Training opt-outL15 · M5

A setting that stops your conversations being used to improve the vendor’s models.

Off by default on a number of consumer tiers. Lesson 15 has you go and find yours.

FERPAL15 · M5

US federal law protecting student education records.

Applies to anyone at FLCC handling student data - including inside a chatbot.

Zero-retentionL15 · M5

An arrangement in which the vendor does not store your inputs at all.

Usually enterprise-only, and worth knowing exists before you need it.

Lesson 16 · Attribution, Copyright and Honest Work

4 terms

AttributionL16 · M5

Crediting a source you drew on.

A different obligation from disclosure, and often confused with it.

DisclosureL16 · M5

Stating that you used AI, and saying how.

The line moves by context - a school, an employer and a journal each draw it differently.

AI-use statementL16 · M5

A short written note describing what AI did in a piece of work.

Increasingly required. You write your own in Lesson 16.

Derivative workL16 · M5

Something based on existing copyrighted material.

The genuinely open legal question sitting under image generation.
