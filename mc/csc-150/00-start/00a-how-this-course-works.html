Six kinds of significant learning

L. Dee Fink argued that a course worth taking changes a learner in six ways, and that none sits above the others - each one strengthens the rest. This course was designed with all six in view.

Learning How to Learn

Learn how to learn

Becoming a better student and a self-directed learner - picking up how to keep learning a subject after the course is over.

How this course builds it
  • Every lesson opens with a guess you commit to before you are allowed to read on, and closes by replaying it. You find out what you actually thought.
  • A retrieval deck at the start of each lesson pulls three questions from earlier lessons - never today’s - because remembering is a skill that improves with practice, and this page says so out loud.
  • The exit check tells you where each question will come back. The spacing is not hidden machinery; you can watch it work.
  • The Learning Hub re-asks the four confidence questions from Week 1 whenever you like, and shows you the change.

Integration

Integrate skills and ideas

Connecting ideas to each other, and connecting what is learned here to the rest of your life and work.

How this course builds it
  • The Apply task in most lessons uses your own week - a prompt you actually wrote (Anatomy of a Prompt), a repair you actually made (Iteration and Repair), three tasks from your own work (The Verification Habit), the tool you use most (Your Data, Their Servers).
  • Your AI Practice assembles every module’s decisions into one page you keep: what you use these tools for, what you never paste, how you check, how you disclose.
  • What This Even Is starts by placing twelve systems you already use into four rings - so the course connects to things you did not think of as AI.

Foundational Knowledge

Develop foundational knowledge

Understanding and remembering the information and ideas the rest of the learning is built on.

How this course builds it
  • A full vocabulary in the Learning Hub, each term with a plain definition, a why-it-matters line and the lesson it arrives in. Nothing is hidden - a glossary you cannot look ahead in is not a glossary.
  • Module 1 builds the mental model first - what these tools are (What This Even Is), how they produce a word (The Next Word Machine), why they lose the thread (Why It Wanders) - before asking you to do anything with them.
  • The Tokenizer shows you the real tokens a model reads, running in your own browser, so the idea is something you have seen rather than been told.

Build and apply skills

Caring

Care about learning

Coming to care about the subject - developing new interests, feelings or values that make you want to keep going.

How this course builds it
  • A reflection journal in the Learning Hub with prompts about specific moments - “where did you catch yourself trusting an output you shouldn’t have?” - rather than general impressions. Entries are dated, kept, and yours to export.
  • Your AI Practice asks a values question first: of everything in this course, which single habit will you still be doing in six months?
  • Honesty is the price of entry, not a deduction. Work without an AI-use statement is handed back to finish rather than marked down, and the course holds itself to the same rule: its illustrations say when they are stylized, and captured model output is never tidied.

Human Dimension

Learn about yourself and others

Learning something important about yourself and about other people - how the subject changes how you see both.

How this course builds it
  • Where You’re Starting From asks where you stand before the course begins, and Your AI Practice asks again. The difference is yours to read.
  • Choosing a Model, Blind asks you to name your favorite tool, then hides the names and lets you vote blind. The reveal is about you, not about the models.
  • Two lessons turn to the other person: whose voice it is (Voice and Audio) and who deserves to know AI was involved (Attribution, Copyright and Honest Work).
  • Checkpoint routes vary by temperament - a written brief, a recorded walkthrough, a built artifact - so choosing one is an act of knowing how you work best.

What is actually graded

Graded: five module checkpoints, a handful of discussions and labs along the way, and the final capstone. Not graded: the Apply tasks, the opening predictions, the warm-up decks, the exit questions, the Week 1 inventory, and everything in the Learning Hub.

Roughly four fifths of what you do in this course is ungraded on purpose - low-stakes practice is where the learning happens, and grading it would only teach you to guess safely. The Apply tasks are the clearest case: nobody collects them, and the checkpoints are built out of them. Skip the practice and the checkpoint is where you find out. There is an optional workbook for each module if you would rather do that thinking on paper - also not collected, also not graded.

Can I use AI to do the work in the AI course?

Mostly yes, on two conditions: you say so, and you can defend it. Using a model to draft, to check your reasoning, or to explain something I explained badly is fine and frankly expected. Using it to skip the verification exercises is the one thing that genuinely costs you, because the verification is the skill - it is the thing that will still be worth something when today’s tools have all been replaced. Lesson 16 covers how to write the disclosure. Until then, a sentence at the end of the assignment is plenty.

Setup

Five things, about ten minutes

The verification prompt

In exactly three sentences, explain what a "knowledge cutoff" is to someone who has never used an AI tool before. Then tell me your own knowledge cutoff date, and say how confident you are that the date is correct.

What a good response looks like: three sentences, a plain explanation, then a date. What is more interesting: whether it hedges on the date. Models are often unreliable about their own cutoff, and watching one be uncertain about itself is a better introduction to Module 3 than anything I could write here. Hang on to whatever it says - you will need it in Lesson 5.

Introduce yourself

One post on the discussion board, then two replies. I know. But this is an online course and a room where nobody knows anybody is a worse room, so let’s get it over with efficiently.

Four short answers
Fill in at least the first two.
Paste this into the board

Edit it however you like - it should sound like you, not like a form.

Then reply to two people

A reply that says “great post!” helps nobody and we both know it. Reply to someone whose skepticism you share, or whose hoped-for use case is close to yours - and say why. Two sentences is plenty. If a thread starts, so much the better; several of these turn into the examples I use later in the course.
