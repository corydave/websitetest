Where Bias Comes From

Three entry points, three different fixes.

Beat 1 · Summary
Overview

Bias in AI output is not one problem with one cause. It enters through the training data, through the human feedback that shaped the model, and through the way you framed the question - and the fixes are different in each case. Rather than assert any of that, this lesson has you build a controlled probe: change exactly one word, and read the difference.

Beat 5 · Content

01 Three entry points, and only one of them is the data

Bias in this context is not an accusation about anyone’s intentions. It is a technical description: a systematic skew in output, in a consistent direction, that is unintended. Systematic is the operative word. A single odd answer is noise but a skew that shows up every time you run the same comparison is a property of the system.

1 The training data. The model learned from an enormous amount of text that people wrote, and people wrote it in a world with a history. If nurses were overwhelmingly described one way and surgeons another across everything it read, that association is now part of what makes a continuation likely. Nothing was decided - the dataset was skewed.

2 The human feedback. After the initial training, models are tuned with Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback - people are shown pairs of outputs and asked which is better, and the model is pushed toward what they preferred. This fixes a great deal, and it introduces a second layer, because now the model is also reproducing the preferences of whoever was hired to do the rating, on the rubric they were given. Models that hedge in a particular way or refuse in a particular way, are most likely reflecting this layer.

3 Your framing. This is the one most don’t think about and the only one you control completely. “What are the benefits of X” and “what are the drawbacks of X” will both come back confident, thorough and one-sided, and neither answer will mention that you asked a leading question. Lesson 7 explained the mechanism: what is in the context steers what comes out, and your question is the context.

The reason to separate the three is that the fixes are completely different. The first is a research problem you cannot touch. The second is a vendor decision you can only choose around by switching tools. The third you can fix in the next thirty seconds, by asking the question the other way and reading both answers.

Beat 6 · Content

02 Controlled probes: change one variable

“It feels biased” is not a claim anyone can act on. A probe turns it into one.

The method is the oldest one there is. Write two prompts that are identical except for a single variable. Run both. Compare.

Two rules make the difference between a probe and an anecdote:

* Change exactly one thing. If you also reworded the sentence, shortened it, or ran the two in different tools, you have basis of comparison.

* Run the control. The same prompt twice will not give you the same answer, because of temperature, and you need to know how much things move when nothing has changed.

Beat 7 · Content

03 Biased is not the same as wrong

These get treated as one problem and they need different responses.

A wrong output has a truth value and fails it. The date is incorrect, the citation is invented, the code does not run. You can catch it by checking, and the fix is to correct it or discard it. Everything in Lessons 8 and 9 was about wrong.

A biased output is frequently, maddeningly, not wrong. Every sentence in it can be defensible on its own. The skew is in what was selected, what was assumed, what was left out, and which register was chosen - and no fact-check catches any of that, because there is no fact to check. Reading it more carefully does not help either. You have to compare it to something.

That is why the probe is the technique. Bias is only visible as a difference, so surfacing it requires a second output to hold it against. One answer, however long you stare at it, will not tell you if bias exists.

And a caution, because this lesson can leave people in a worse place than it found them - not every difference is a bias! The control pair exists to make that concrete: some of what you see is sampling. Some is a real difference in the world that the model reflected accurately, and calling that bias is its own kind of error. The claim worth making is narrow and it is strong: I changed this one thing, and this is what moved. Say that much and you can show your working.

Which entry point is it?

Does it change when you reword the question neutrally? Then it was your framing, and it is already fixed.

Does it change when you run the same probe in a different vendor’s tool? Then you are probably looking at the feedback layer, because the training corpora overlap far more than the tuning does.

Does it hold across tools and across framings? Then it is in the data, and you cannot fix it. You can only know it is there and decide what to do about it, which is what the next lesson is for.

The one that gets people

Asking the model whether it is biased is not a probe. You will get a thoughtful, well-organized, entirely generated paragraph about the importance of fairness, produced by exactly the same process as everything else. It has no privileged view of its own weights. Run the pair instead.

Beat 9 · Apply
Do this before the next lesson

Run your own probe pair

Run your control first: the same prompt twice, word for word, in two fresh conversations. Write down what moved when nothing changed. That is your floor, and everything below is measured against it.
Write your own pair in a domain you know something about. Identical prompts except for one variable. Not two variables, not a reworded sentence - one.
Run both. Then run both again, in fresh conversations. A difference that does not show up the second time is noise wearing a finding’s clothes, and reporting that is a complete lab.
If it did reproduce, rewrite your question as neutrally as you can and run the pair once more. If the difference disappears, it came from your framing - the one entry point you control.
Write three sentences: what you changed, what moved, and which entry point you think it came from. Then one more: what would have to be true for you to be wrong.
Reflection: Be sure to log this in your workbook (if you are using it).

Two questions

These enter the course item bank right now and come back at you unannounced, in later lessons. Don’t worry! This is the design, not a threat! Seriously. No threat.

You reword a question from “benefits of” to “drawbacks of” and get two confident, one-sided answers. Which entry point is that?

Framing. It is the only one of the three you can fix immediately, and the only one nobody else can fix for you.

This resurfaces in Lessons 15 and 17

Why does a controlled probe need a control pair - the same prompt run twice?

To find the floor. Temperature means two runs of an identical prompt already differ. Without knowing by how much, you cannot tell a finding from noise.
