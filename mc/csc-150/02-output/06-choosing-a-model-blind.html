Beat 5 · Content

01 Take the labels off

Ask somebody which AI tool is best and you will get an answer with real confidence behind it. Ask how they know, and it is almost always some mixture of what they tried first, what their workplace pays for, and what they have read about. None of that is a comparison. All that is is a habit with a rationale attached afterwards.

A blind comparison removes the one variable you cannot control for by trying harder: what you already believe about the name on the box. Your bias. Two answers to the same prompt. No labels. You pick. Only then do the names drop. The gap between which one you picked and which one you expected to pick is the entire point, and it might not be comfortable for you. That’s ok - models change all the time. If you had a bad experience a few months ago with a model, chances are it has improved dramatically since then.

Three rounds below, and they are deliberately different kinds of work. Vote on all three before you look at the summary.

02 What a leaderboard can and cannot tell you

        

          
Those public arenas aggregate millions of votes into a ranked table. The ranking is usually an Elo rating - the same system chess uses, where beating a strong opponent moves you more than beating a weak one. It is a good measure of one thing: which model a crowd of anonymous voters preferred, on the prompts that crowd happened to type.

          
That is worth something and it is not worth what people take from it. A leaderboard position is an average over somebody else’s task mix. When the top four are within a couple of points of each other, the honest reading is that they are indistinguishable for most purposes and the ordering is noise.

          
The board most people mean is arena.ai, which is where the blind-vote idea you just did by hand actually happens at scale. It ran as Chatbot Arena, then as LMArena, and became arena.ai in January 2026.

          
But a preference board is only one of the two things people mean by “the rankings”, and they are not the same animal.

          
A benchmark is a fixed set of questions with known answers, run automatically, scored as a percentage. Nobody votes. That makes it repeatable and comparable in a way preference votes are not - and it introduces a different problem, which is that a benchmark can only ask what somebody thought to write down. Two boards can rank the same two models in opposite orders and both be reporting honestly, because they are measuring different things.

          
The trap specific to benchmarks is the table in a vendor’s own announcement. Those are real tests with real numbers, and they are also chosen by the party being tested. A benchmark a model does badly on does not appear in the post. The independently-run boards are the ones worth your time, and the checkpoint for this module will have you look a model up on several of them and watch its rank move.

          
Which brings us back to your three rounds. If one model did not sweep them, you have produced the finding this lesson exists for: best is not something a model has. It is something a model has for a task, and the task changes it. That is also why the practical skill here is not memorizing which tool is ahead this month - that will have changed by the time you finish the course, and the names on the arena have already changed once while this lesson was being written.

          
One caution about running your own. When you compare on a task where you cannot check the answer, you are rating fluency, and fluency is exactly what these systems are built to produce. Preferring the better-written answer is not the same as preferring the more accurate one, and Lesson 8 is about what happens when those two come apart.

Before you type anything into a public arena

    
arena.ai states plainly that your conversations are passed to the model providers and may be made public. That is the deal that makes the site free, and it is a fair deal - but it means a public arena is the wrong place for anything that is not yours to publish.

    
No student work, no student names, no grades, no patient or client information, nothing under FERPA or HIPAA, nothing your employer would call confidential. Write a prompt that resembles your real task without carrying any of its content, and use that instead. This applies to every free AI service, and we come back to it properly in Module 5.

  





  

  

    
Four things a leaderboard does not measure

    
What it costs you. Ranking is preference, not price. The model two rows down may be a tenth of the cost and indistinguishable on your work.

    
How long it takes. A model that is three points better and forty seconds slower is worse for anything you do while waiting.

    
What it refuses. Refusal rates vary a lot between models and never appear in the headline number. If your subject is medical, legal or security, this is the column you actually need.

    
Your task. The score is an aggregate over whatever a crowd of strangers happened to ask. If your work looks nothing like that mix, the ordering was computed for somebody else.

  





  

  

    
The ten-minute version

    
Take a real task you did last week. Run it through two tools. Paste both answers into a document with the names stripped off, leave it an hour, then come back and pick. That is a blind comparison, it costs you ten minutes, and it will tell you more about which tool suits your work than any ranking will.

C. Often enough to be uncomfortable, and it swings hard by task. In a well-matched pair the blind preference is close to a coin flip, which is a strange thing to sit with if you have been recommending one of them to colleagues.
The mechanism is not mysterious. With the label showing, you are rating the answer and the brand together, and you cannot separate them by trying to - that is what makes it a bias rather than an opinion. Take the label off and the only thing left to rate is the text. Your own three rounds are above; if one model did not sweep them, you have just produced the evidence yourself.

         Do this before the next lesson
        
Three pictures, one winner

      

      

        

          
Copy the prompt above exactly as written, without improving it.

          
Run it in three image generators. Use ChatGPT and Gemini, then pick a third yourself - Grok, Meta AI, Copilot, Firefly, whatever you already have or want to try. One prompt, one attempt each, no rewording and no follow-ups.

          
Rank the three images a completely different way: which one would you actually use? First, second, third. No scoring, no justifying, just the one you would pick.

          
Now score each image out of six, one point per specification actually met: pencil sketch rather than a photo, exactly three mugs, all handles facing left, middle mug red and the other two white, a legible sign reading OPEN, no people. Half marks are not a thing - if you are arguing with yourself about whether it counts, it does not count.

          
Put the two rankings side by side. Do they match? Say so plainly either way.

          
Which of the three did you reach for first, before you read any of this? Did it win either ranking?
