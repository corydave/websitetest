Case Studies

Ten short cases about organizations and their data. Some are clear problems, some are fine, and some are hard to call. You’ll highlight any issues, identify why they are issues, make a call on each one, and download a PDF of your work. About 45 to 60 minutes.

Two that really happened

Neither of these involved AI. Both are about what a system does with people’s data when nobody changes the settings.

Strava’s heatmap

Strava is a fitness app that records where people run and ride. In 2017 it published a global “heatmap”: a picture of every route its users had logged, all over the world. In early 2018, an Australian student named Nathan Ruser noticed something in the empty places. In remote parts of Afghanistan, Iraq, Somalia and Syria, there were bright little loops where there should have been nothing. They were soldiers jogging laps around their bases. The map showed where the bases were, and even the routes people walked inside them.

Users had been opted in through settings that were confusing to find. Most of those soldiers never chose to share anything.

Venmo’s public payments

Venmo, the payment app, made every payment public by default for years: who paid whom, and the note they typed with it. Researchers later looked at 389 million of those public payments and found that about one in ten revealed something sensitive, including drug use, health conditions, phone numbers and even passwords. In 2018, PayPal, which owns Venmo, settled with the Federal Trade Commission over claims that Venmo misled people about how private their payments were.

The pattern

In both cases the data wasn’t stolen. The system shared it, because sharing was the default and most people never changed it. The opposite approach is called privacy by default: start private, and let people choose to share. Watch for the same pattern in the cases below, especially when AI is the thing doing the sharing.

How this works

  1. Read it, and look for data: what kind it is, whose it is, where it goes, and who can see it.
  2. Highlight what matters. Select some words, then press Highlight what I selected. Or pick a sentence from the list and press Highlight this sentence. That way works with a keyboard, too.

The cases

title | Reminders That Know Too Much
org | Riverside Family Dental
verdict | problem
---
Jo runs the front desk at Riverside Family Dental, a practice with three dentists. Every afternoon she texts patients to remind them about the next day's appointments, and it eats up an hour.

She found a free AI chat app on her phone that writes friendly reminder texts. To save time, she photographs the next day's patient intake forms, all of them, and uploads the photos. The forms list each patient's full name, date of birth, home address, insurance member number, current medications, and the reason for the visit.

The app writes great reminders, like: "Hi Dennis! Just a reminder about your root canal follow-up tomorrow at 9:30." Jo copies each one into the office texting system. Nobody else at the practice knows she's using it, and she has never looked at the app's settings.
title | The Menu Posts
org | Hearth & Crumb Bakery
verdict | fine
---
Lena, from the bakery in Week 1's Case Files, learned her lesson. She still uses a free AI chat to write her weekly social media posts, but now she only pastes in the public product sheet: item names, descriptions and prices that are already on the website and in the window.

Her instructions now include a "never say" list: no allergen claims, no health claims, and no prices that aren't on the sheet. She reads every post out loud before it goes up, and she has caught two small mistakes that way.

She also turned off the app's chat history, though she admits she only did it because a friend told her to.
title | The Roster Notes
org | Valley United Youth Soccer
verdict | gray
---
Andre volunteers as registrar for Valley United, a youth soccer league with 300 kids. Every season he has to split each age group into balanced teams, and every season it takes him a whole weekend.

This year he exports the registration spreadsheet and pastes it into the AI tool his employer provides for his day job. It's a business plan that promises not to train on what he types. He asks it to make balanced teams by age and skill rating.

The spreadsheet has each child's name, age, birthday, school, last season's skill rating, and a "notes" column that parents filled in. Some notes say things like "carpools with the Okafors." Others mention a child's ADHD medication, a custody arrangement, or "dad is not allowed to pick up."

The teams it makes are well balanced, and Andre is home by lunch.
title | The Upsell Engine
org | Lakeview Credit Union
verdict | problem
---
Lakeview Credit Union's marketing team wanted to send members more personal offers. A vendor sold them an AI assistant that connects to the credit union's new data lake: every account balance, every transaction, every loan application, and every call-center note from the last ten years.

The team asked it to "find members who would benefit from our debt consolidation loan" and draft an email to each one. It picked members with frequent overdrafts, payments to payday lenders, and charges at gambling sites, and wrote emails that mentioned them: "We noticed you've had a tough few months..."

The emails went out automatically to 2,300 members the same afternoon. Nobody on the marketing team read them first. Several members called to ask how the marketing department knew what they spent their money on.
title | Minutes in Plain English
org | City of Ashford Parks and Recreation
verdict | fine
---
The Ashford Parks and Recreation department posts its board meeting minutes on the city's website, as the law requires. Hardly anyone reads them: they're long, formal, and full of motions and seconds.

Devon, the department's communications assistant, uses the city's approved AI tool to turn each set of published minutes into a short, plain-language summary for the monthly newsletter: what was decided, what's coming up, and how residents can weigh in.

Devon checks every summary against the minutes before it goes out, especially anything about money, dates, or what was actually voted on. The newsletter always links to the full minutes.
title | Anonymous Exit Interviews
org | Kessler Manufacturing
verdict | gray
---
Kessler Manufacturing has 40 employees. When someone leaves, HR holds an exit interview and types up notes. After a rough year with eight departures, the owner asked HR to look for patterns.

Renee in HR uses the company's approved AI tool, which the company pays for and which doesn't train on its data. Before pasting the notes in, she deletes every name and replaces it with "Employee 1," "Employee 2," and so on.

The notes still say things like "second-shift welder, 22 years here, left after a dispute with the shift supervisor over overtime" and "only woman on the maintenance crew; said she was tired of the comments." The AI's summary uses those details as examples under each pattern, and Renee sends it to the owner and all four department heads.
title | The Helpful Pickup Bot
org | Northside Food Pantry
verdict | problem
---
The Northside Food Pantry gets the same phone question all day: "When's my next pickup?" A volunteer who is good with computers built a chatbot for the pantry's website over a weekend to answer it.

To make it work, he gave the bot the pantry's sign-in spreadsheet: every household's name, address, household size, pickup dates, and whether they receive SNAP benefits. Anyone who visits the website can chat with it. There's no login.

It works. A client types her name and gets her pickup date. So does her neighbor, when he types her name. So does anyone who asks, "Who picked up food last week?"
title | Listing Photos
org | Fieldstone Realty
verdict | gray
---
Nate, a real estate agent at Fieldstone Realty, uses an AI tool his brokerage approved to write listing descriptions. He uploads the photos of each house and gets back a warm, detailed description in seconds.

The photos are the same ones that go on the public listing. In one house, they show the children's names painted on their bedroom doors, a weekly schedule on the fridge with soccer practice and pickup times, mail on the kitchen counter, and the keypad for the home's security system by the front door.

The description is lovely and doesn't mention any of that. Nate posts the listing with all 32 photos.
title | The Real Spreadsheet
org | Bright Line Heating & Cooling
verdict | gray
---
Tasha, from the heating company in Week 1's Case Files, needed another formula: this one adds a late fee once an invoice is 30 days overdue. This time she was in a hurry.

Instead of making three made-up rows like last time, she uploaded the real invoice spreadsheet to a free AI chat on her personal account: 640 customers' names, addresses, phone numbers, and how much each one owes.

The formula it gave back was perfect. She tested it on rows due yesterday, today and tomorrow, like she learned to. Then she deleted the chat.
title | The Firm's Own Tool
org | Morrow & Lee
verdict | fine
---
Morrow & Lee is a law firm with 12 lawyers. Last year, after months of review, the firm signed a contract with an AI vendor for a legal research and drafting tool. The contract says the vendor can't train on the firm's data, must delete it on request, and must keep it in the United States. The firm's IT person set it up so only staff can log in, and every use is logged.

Ana, a paralegal, uses it to summarize a 300-page deposition transcript for an upcoming case, a job that used to take her two days. The transcript is full of the client's medical history and finances.

The summary goes to the supervising attorney, who checks it against the transcript before anything is used in the case. Ana never uses any other AI tool for client work.

What matters. The intake forms are restricted data: health information, insurance numbers and dates of birth. They went into a free app on a personal phone that nobody at the practice approved, and nobody knows what it keeps. In the U.S., dental offices are generally covered by HIPAA, the federal health privacy law. The reminder only needed a first name and a time. Everything else was extra, and the extra is the dangerous part.

Minor error, big issue. The reminder text itself says “root canal.” Anyone who glances at Dennis’s phone now knows why he’s going to the dentist.

What would fix it. The task is fine; the data isn’t. Send only a first name and a time, through the office’s own texting system, which can probably send reminders without any AI at all. And never photograph patient forms on a personal phone.

What matters. Everything Lena pastes in is public. For public data, any tool is fine, including a free one. Her never-say list and her read-aloud check are exactly the controls from Case Files, and they’re working: two caught mistakes.

This is Delegate done right. The AI drafts, and a person with eyes on every word decides what goes out.

The red herring. Turning off chat history is a nice habit, but it doesn’t matter much here, because there’s nothing private to keep. If you marked this a problem because the app is free, look at the level first: the level decides the rule.

What’s good. A business tool that doesn’t train on his data, and a reasonable job for AI. Balancing teams is tedious, and nothing bad happens if a team is a little uneven.

What tips it gray. The notes column holds restricted information about children: health, custody, and a safety warning. The AI needed none of it to balance teams. And Andre used his employer’s tool for something that isn’t his employer’s work. That may break his workplace’s rules, and it puts 300 children’s details into his employer’s account, where its administrators could see them.

What would fix it. Before pasting, delete the notes, school and birthday columns, and use first names or initials. Ask the league whether it has its own tool. And “dad is not allowed to pick up” is a real safety need. It belongs with the coaches, not in an AI chat.

What matters. The blast radius was the whole data lake. Marketing could reach ten years of every member’s transactions, which marketing never needed. Members gave the credit union that data to run their accounts, not to be sold to. And the emails turned it into something worse: guesses about money trouble and gambling, said out loud.

The 4D mistake. This was treated as Deputize, with no human reading anything, when it should have been Delegate at most. It went out under the credit union’s name, to 2,300 people, and it can’t be taken back.

What would fix it. Data governance first: marketing gets only what it needs, like which members are eligible, not the transactions behind it. Never mention spending in a marketing message. And a person reads before anything is sent.

What matters. The minutes are already public, by law. The tool is the one the city approved. This is Delegate: the AI drafts, and Devon checks every decision, number and date against the source. It’s the same control the credit union newsletter needed in Case Files.

A nice extra. Linking to the full minutes means anyone can check the summary themselves.

If you flagged the names in the minutes: board members and the people who speak at public meetings are part of a public record. That’s not the same as private data.

What’s good. An approved tool, a fair question, and Renee really did try: she removed the names.

What tips it gray. Remember Sweeney: taking the names out is not the same as making data anonymous. In a 40-person company, “the only woman on the maintenance crew” is a name, and so is “second-shift welder, 22 years here.” The summary went to all four department heads, possibly including the supervisor in that dispute. And “tired of the comments” may be a harassment concern that needs HR to act, not just a line in a pattern.

What would fix it. Strip roles, shifts and years of service too, not only names. Report patterns without examples that point at a person. Send it only to the people who need it. Take the harassment concern out of the AI exercise and handle it properly.

What matters. This is Strava and Venmo again: public by default. Who uses a food pantry, where they live, and who receives SNAP is sensitive, and some of it is protected. Now anyone on the internet can ask for it. The blast radius is every client the pantry has, and it can’t be undone.

The 4D call. Don’t, at least for this design. The volunteer meant well, and the question is a good one to automate. The data just never belonged in a public bot.

What would fix it. Answer the question without anyone’s data. “Households with last names A to M pick up in the first week of the month” needs no names at all. Or send each household its own reminder by text, so the answer only goes to the person it belongs to.

The surprise. The AI is the one step that handled this well. It’s an approved tool, it’s Delegate (Nate reads the description), and the description leaves the family’s details out.

What tips it gray. The real exposure is the public listing itself. Thirty-two photos now tell anyone online the children’s names, when the house is empty, and where the security keypad is. Data problems aren’t always AI problems, and a good data map covers every step, not just the AI one.

What would fix it. A photo checklist before anything goes public: names, schedules, mail and security systems out of the shot, or blurred.

What tips it toward a problem. Customer contact details and what each one owes are confidential, and they went into an unapproved free tool on a personal account. Deleting the chat may not delete the data; that’s the retention question from the lesson. And the formula didn’t need a single real row. It needed the column names.

What keeps it gray. No restricted data (no card or bank numbers), a smaller blast radius than the credit union’s, and her testing habit is exactly right.

What would fix it. Two minutes of made-up rows, or just the column headers. The AI needed the shape of the data, not the data.

What matters. This is restricted data, and it’s still fine, because this is the exception from the lesson: “Don’t, unless the organization set up a tool for exactly this.” Morrow & Lee did. A contract with no training, deletion on request and a known location, logins for staff only, a log of every use, and an attorney who checks the work. That’s data governance doing its job.

The 4D call. Delegate: the AI drafts, and a professional with eyes on it decides.

If you called it a problem because the data is restricted: the level decides the rule, and the rule here was built for this data. Restricted doesn’t mean never. It means not without the right setup, and this is the right setup.
