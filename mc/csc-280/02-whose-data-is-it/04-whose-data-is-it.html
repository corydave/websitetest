What counts as personal

Personally identifiable information, or PII, is any information that can point to one specific person. Some of it does that on its own: a name, a Social Security number, an email address, a phone number, a photo of a face.

The rest does it in combination. A ZIP code isn’t personal. Neither is a birth date. Put them together, and add one more fact, and they very often are.

That number comes from Latanya Sweeney, a computer scientist who studies privacy. She found that ZIP code, date of birth and gender were enough to single out about 87 out of every 100 people in the United States.

She proved the point in 1996, when Massachusetts released state employees’ medical records to researchers with the names and Social Security numbers taken out. Sweeney bought voter registration records for $20, matched the three facts, and picked out the governor’s own records.

The lesson

Taking the names out is not the same as making data anonymous. Before you paste anything into an AI, ask: could someone work out who this is? If yes, treat it as personal.

Four levels of sensitive

Most organizations sort their data into a few levels, so nobody has to decide from scratch every time. The names vary from place to place, but nearly every scheme looks something like this. Each piece of data gets a sensitivity level, and the level decides the rule.

    What happens to what you type

    When you paste something into an AI tool, it leaves your computer. What happens next depends on the tool and on the kind of account, and it changes often enough that you should always check before submitting sensitive information:

    1. Does it learn from my chats? Some tools use conversations to train future models unless you switch that off. Business and education plans usually promise not to.
    2. How long is it kept? This is called data retention. Even with training switched off, chats are often stored for a while, and sometimes people at the company can review them.
    3. Who else can see it? Shared links, workspace admins, connected apps.

    You’ll check these three for your own tool in the Lab.

    It happened at Samsung

    In 2023, reports said engineers at Samsung had pasted confidential source code and internal meeting notes into ChatGPT to fix bugs and write up minutes. Samsung told staff in one of its biggest divisions to stop using ChatGPT and similar tools, with firing as a possible penalty. Nobody broke in. Employees typed company secrets into a tool their company hadn’t approved, trying to get their work done faster.

    One big pool

    Organizations collect data in lots of places: email, spreadsheets, the customer system, the shared drive. A data lake is what you get when an organization pours all of it into one big store so it can be searched and analyzed together.

    That’s powerful, and it’s exactly why AI makes it risky. An AI assistant connected to a data lake can reach everything in it: the menu and the payroll, the meeting agenda and the student grades. Deciding who and what is allowed to see which parts is called data governance.

    You met the word in CSC 240. Blast radius is how much harm something can do if it goes wrong: who it reaches, and whether it can be undone. An assistant that can read one folder has a small blast radius. The same assistant connected to the whole data lake has an enormous one, even if it never does anything wrong on purpose.

    Shrink the blast radius

    Give an AI tool the least data it needs to do the job, not everything it could use. One folder, not the whole drive.

    The 4Ds: how much AI should touch this?

    Sensitivity tells you what the data is. It doesn’t tell you how to work. For that, use the 4Ds, a framework from your instructor. Every task you might hand to AI lands in one of four places.

    Deputize

    When things don’t matter much, usually when you’re using AI for yourself. Let it run. If it’s a little off, nobody gets hurt.

    Delegate

    A task AI can do fairly well, but you’ll comb through the output and put your own spin on it. You need eyes on the content before it goes anywhere.

    Duet

    You and the AI partner up, push each other, and get somewhere neither would alone. This is the sweet spot.

    Don’t

    When AI shouldn’t be used at all: the data is too sensitive, the stakes are too high, or the work is the point.

    The data level and the blast radius push a task toward one D or another. Public data and a small blast radius? Deputizing is fine. Confidential data, or output that goes out under your organization’s name? Delegate at most, with a person reading every word. Restricted data, or a decision about a person’s job, money or health? Usually Don’t.

    Where would you put these six? Pick a D for each, then compare with our call. These are judgment calls, so if you land somewhere else, make sure you could say why.

      Brainstorming names for your fantasy football team | deputize | It's for you, and nothing rides on it.
