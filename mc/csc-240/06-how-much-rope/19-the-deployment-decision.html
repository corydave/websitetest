The Deployment Decision

Five weeks ago you picked a job you actually do. Since then it has gained a map, rules, files, and a trigger. Today you make the two calls that decide whether it is ready for real life - where it runs, and how much it may do alone - and you write the instructions someone else would need to run it without you.

If your answers are not saving

This page keeps your work in your browser. If you are in a private window, or your browser blocks site storage, nothing will be held between visits - write your answers somewhere else as you go, and use Download at the end.

Bring everything

Have these open beside you. You are not building anything new today - you are deciding about what you already built.

  • instructions.md - its rules (Build 10), and its Files section (Build 13)
  • knowledge.md - the files it reads (Build 13)
  • workflow.md - the handoff that starts itself (Build 16). Open it in the Workflow Builder if you want to look at it again.
  • Your answer from the Lab: would you run it locally?

Missing one? Use what you have and say so in your hand-in. A Capstone with an honest gap beats one with an invented piece.

Decision 1: where it runs

Hosted, local, or part of each. Decide, and give the reason that tipped it. The best reasons point at something specific:

  • Your data. Open knowledge.md. What is in it? Would you be comfortable with all of it on a company’s computers?
  • Quality. In the Lab, how did the local model do on your job, prompt 2?
  • Speed, cost and hardware. What do you actually have, and how long can the person waiting afford to wait?

Then say what you give up. Every choice costs something - a hosted build gives up control of the data; a local one gives up some sharpness. Naming the cost is how you show the decision was a decision.

Where it runs

What shape is your data?

Most of your files are probably writing: rules, notes, examples. But some jobs run on tables - a roster, a sign-up sheet, a price list. A table with named columns is Structured data, and AI handles it best when it knows exactly what each column means.

If any of your data is a table, write down its columns and what each one holds. That list is the table’s Schema, and it belongs in your runbook - it is the first thing someone taking over will ask. If none of your data is a table, say so; that is an answer too.

Decision 2: how much rope

List every action your build takes - every time it sends, posts, adds, books or changes something. Your workflow.md has them; so does any agent’s list of tools.

For each, pick a setting from the lesson’s Autonomy Dial, and name the mistake it protects against:

  • Suggest - it drafts, a person approves.
  • Act, then report - it acts, and a person reads the record.
  • Act silently - it acts, and nobody looks. This needs a reason you would be happy to say out loud to the person it goes wrong for.

    Guardrails that hold

    Now list at least two guardrails - the limits that stop your build doing something it should not. For each, say where it lives:

    • Enforced: outside the model. A review step, an ask first switch, an account that cannot send to anyone outside your team.
    • Only asked: a sentence in instructions.md.

    Both kinds are useful. But go back to your rope table: any action whose mistake would be costly needs at least one guardrail that is enforced. Remember the web page that talked Rosa’s agent into forwarding a stranger’s request - words can argue with words.

      How you will know what it did

      Say what your build records, where, who reads it, and how often. In the Workflow Builder, that is Activity and Monitor; in a real tool, it is usually called run history.

      Then one more line: what would make you stop it? Three bad drafts in a row? One email to the wrong person? A real audit trail comes with a rule for when you pull the plug.

      Take one screenshot of your audit trail - Monitor from your Build 16 workflow, or your real tool’s run history - to hand in with this.

      Write the runbook

      A runbook is the page someone reads when you are on vacation and the thing needs running. Fill in each part below - plain sentences, no jargon you would not explain to a new coworker. The page builds runbook.md as you type.

      What it does, in two sentences. What arrives, and what comes out.

      What you need to run it: the tool or tools, the accounts, the files, and where it runs.

      How to start it, and how to stop it - fast. Stopping is the part people forget to write down.

      What to check every time it runs, before anything goes out.

      When it fails: who gets alerted, and what they should do first.

      What it must never do - and which of those are enforced, not just asked.

      How to tell what it did: where the record is, and the rule for when to stop it.

      Keeping it current: who updates instructions.md and knowledge.md, how often, and which version this is.

      The cold read
      
    
      
      
      A runbook you have only read yourself has not been tested - you fill every gap from memory without noticing. So hand it to a stranger.
      

      Copy your runbook.md. Open a new chat in any AI tool - not one of your Projects, so it knows nothing about you - and paste this, then your runbook:
      
      
      You are taking over this job tomorrow, and I will not be reachable. Read the runbook below. List every question you would need answered before you could run it safely and correctly. Number them. Do not answer them yourself and do not guess - only list what is missing or unclear.
      
      
      
        
      
      
      
      
      
      Count the questions. Pick the one that matters most, fix your runbook so it is answered, and say what you changed. (Zero questions is suspicious - try a second tool before you believe it.)

      How it is graded
      

      Five criteria, up to four points each, twenty points in all.
      
    
      CriterionNot yet - 0Meets - 2Strong - 4
      Where it runsNo decision, or no reasonA decision, with a reason tied to your data, quality or hardwareAlso names what the choice gives up - and uses what you saw in the Lab
      How much ropeActions with no settingEvery action has a setting and a reasonEach reason names the mistake it protects against, and nothing acts silently without a reason you could say out loud
      Guardrails that holdNone, or only sentences in the instructionsAt least two, each marked enforced or only askedEvery costly action has a guardrail that is enforced, not just asked
      You can see what it didNo record namedWhat is recorded, where, and who reads it - with a screenshotAlso how often, and the rule for when to stop it
      Someone else could run itNo runbook, or parts left emptyEvery part of the runbook filled in plain sentencesThe cold read done, and the gap that mattered most fixed
      
    
      The AI-use statement is not a criterion
      Work without one comes back unmarked so you can add it - not marked down, just not marked yet. Same rule as every Build.
