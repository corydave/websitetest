Part 1 · The idea

Write it once, use it every time

Without a plan, memory, a harness, codified instructions, every chat starts blank. You explain the job, the format, the rules - and next Monday you explain them again.

A persistent assistant is that explanation, written down once and saved where the AI reads it at the start of every chat. Underneath it is the same two things:

  • Instructions - what it is for, how to answer, what it must never do
  • Files - the facts it should work from (that is next week)

That is why this week starts with a file you own, instructions.md, and only then puts it into a tool. When the tool changes its name - and they do - your file still works.

Part 2 · How it works

Read, not learned

Here is the part that surprises people. A persistent assistant does not learn your job. At the start of every chat it reads its instructions from the top, as if for the first time, and works from that.

So if you correct it in a chat - “no, put the dates in bold” - that correction lives in that one chat. Next week it is gone. To change what it does, change the file. Then paste the new version into the tool.

Some tools also keep a separate “memory” of things you have said. Treat that as a bonus you cannot see or check. Anything the job depends on goes in the instructions, where you can read it.

Part 3 · Try it

The Scope Dial

Scoping is deciding what one assistant is for - and, just as important, what it is not for. It is tempting to make one assistant that does everything. Try it and see what that costs.

Below are two instruction files for Maria’s food pantry. The narrow one is the file you saw in Module 1. The wide one is what people write when they want one assistant for everything.

  1. Open a new chat in any AI tool. Paste the narrow file, then send the three requests below, one at a time.
  2. Open a second new chat. Paste the wide file and send the same three requests.
  3. For each request, mark which assistant followed the pantry’s rules better.
Narrow 
# Pantry Shift Helper

## What you are for
You help Maria turn the weekly volunteer sign-up sheet into a shift schedule and reminder emails. Nothing else.

## How to answer
- Group volunteers by day, then by role: intake, stocking, distribution.
- Mark any shift with fewer than 3 people **SHORT**.
- Reminder emails: warm, plain, under 80 words.

## Never
- Never invent a volunteer, a time or a shift.
- Never send anything. Draft only.

## When you are unsure
Say which line of the sheet confused you, and ask.
If a request is not about shifts or reminders, say so and suggest Maria ask somewhere else.

What the dial shows

Neither assistant is “smarter”. The narrow one has rules for this job and nothing to distract it, so you can check its work against those rules. The wide one has no rules to check against - every answer is a fresh guess at what you wanted.

That is why the course builds one assistant for one job. An assistant that does everything does nothing you can rely on.

Part 4 · Sort it

Enforced, or only asked?

The Never list is the most important part of an instruction file - and it has a weakness. Writing “never do X” does not make X impossible. It asks the AI not to. Most of the time it listens. Not always.

Some rules are different. If the AI was never given the phone numbers, it cannot leak them. If it has no way to send email, it cannot send one. Those rules are enforced by how the thing is set up, not by how nicely you asked.

Sort the eight rules below. For each, decide: is it enforced - the AI cannot break it - or only asked?

    Sort all eight to see your score.

    Don’t forget!

    For every Never that really matters, ask: can I make this impossible instead of just asking? Do not give it the data. Do not give it the button. Keep that step a “Me” step. Ask nicely for the rest - and check.

    When “only asked” was not enough

    In 2024, a Canadian tribunal ordered Air Canada to honor a refund policy its website chatbot had made up. The airline argued the chatbot was responsible for its own words. The tribunal disagreed: the company owns what its assistant says.

    In 2023, a car dealer’s chatbot was talked into “agreeing” to sell a new SUV for one dollar. The dealer did not honor it, but the screenshots went everywhere. Nothing in how the chatbot was set up made that answer impossible.

    What should it refuse?

    Every assistant you build this week has a Never list. The hard part is not writing it - it is deciding what goes on it. Start with people: who could be hurt, embarrassed or misled if this assistant got it wrong? Their protection goes first on the list, and if you can, make it enforced.
