From Prompt to Process

This week you stop asking AI one thing at a time and start writing down the whole job.

~40 minutes 1 tool to try Nothing to hand in here
A question from CSC 150

At the very end of CSC 150 you were asked which habit you would still be doing in six months, and told you would be asked again here. So: are you? Whatever your answer, notice what a habit is - a little process that lives in your head. This course is about writing those processes down, so they can be checked, shared, and partly handed to an AI.

Part 1 · The idea

One prompt, or a process?

Most people use AI one ask at a time. Sometimes that works. When it does not, you cannot tell which part went wrong - so you read everything, fix it by hand, and try again the nex time from scratch.

One big prompt

Here is this week's volunteer sign-up sheet. Sort everyone into shifts, tell me which shifts are short, figure out who I should call to fill the gaps, and write a reminder email to every volunteer.

The same job, as a process

StepWho
1. Download Monday’s sign-up sheetMaria
2. Sort sign-ups into shifts by day and roleAI
3. Flag any shift with fewer than three peopleAI
4. Decide who to call to fill the gapsMaria
5. Draft one reminder email per volunteerAI
6. Send the remindersMaria

That is Maria’s job from page 1. The process is not more work. It is the same work, with three things the big prompt does not have:

  • Every step does one job, so when something goes wrong you know where.
  • Every step has an owner. The decisions Maria should make stay with Maria.
  • Every AI step can be checked - before the next step builds on it.

A job written down like this is a workflow: a named set of steps that gets one job done again and again, with AI doing some of the steps and you doing the rest.

Part 2 · Decomposition

Small enough to check

Breaking a job into steps has a name - decomposition - and one goal: steps small enough that you can check each one. Four rules of thumb:

  1. Start each step with a verb. Sort, draft, decide, send. If you cannot find the verb, the step is not a step yet.
  2. If a step says “then”, it is two steps. Draft the email, then post it hides a handoff in the middle.
  3. Name what comes out. A list, a draft, a decision. The next step needs to know what it is getting.
  4. The one-minute test. Could you tell whether this step went right in about a minute? If not, it is too big. Split it.
Part 3 · Handoffs

Where it breaks: handoffs

A handoff is any point where work passes from you to the AI, or from the AI back to you. Most workflows break at a handoff, because nobody designed it. It just happened. Three ways it goes wrong:

  • Wrong shape. The AI hands back a friendly paragraph, and the next step needed a list.
  • Lost context. The next step does not know something the last one knew - like which shifts were already full.
  • Nobody looks. A draft goes out because everyone assumed someone else had checked it.

The fix is to design handoffs on purpose: say exactly what gets passed along, in what form, and who looks at it. On Maria’s map, every dashed line is a handoff she thought about.

Part 4 · Checks

A check for every AI step

Every AI step needs a way you would notice it went wrong. It does not have to be heavy. Match the check to the risk - the closer a mistake gets to another person, the harder you look.

Part 6 · Two words

Two words that sound alike

You will meet both. Only one of them is a workflow.

Prompt chaining

The output of one prompt becomes the input of the next, and each prompt does one job. Sort these. Now, from that sorted list, draft the emails. It is a workflow with AI steps back to back - and you can check between them. You will try it in the Lab.

Chain-of-thought

Asking a model to reason step by step inside one answer before it replies. Sometimes it helps; sometimes it only makes the answer longer. Either way it all happens in one turn, so there is no middle for you to check.

Whose work is it?

Once some of the steps are the AI’s, whose work is the result? Yours. Your name goes on it, and the decisions in it are yours. That is why every Build ends with an AI-use statement - which steps were the AI’s, and how you checked them. A good map makes that statement easy to write: it is mostly the AI rows of your table.
